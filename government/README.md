# Caribbean government AI implementation playbook

Practical starting points for ministries, departments, regulators, public bodies and local government across the Caribbean. Prepared for the AI Playbook for the Caribbean and Latin America, October 2026.

These are **proposed implementation patterns**, not claims that any named government has deployed them. The sample outputs are fictional. They are not legal, clinical, engineering or emergency instructions. Each administration must validate its own authority, law, language, data and operating conditions before a pilot.

## Three levels, not a race to autonomy

| Level | Intended work | Minimum starting position | Release gate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1: Assistive | Summarise, draft, translate and explain approved material for staff review | A named service owner, approved source material, secure approved tool and time for review | Every output is checked before use; no autonomous decisions or publication |
| 2: Workflow | Extract, organise, route and suggest next steps in a controlled service process | Level 1 controls plus reliable records, access permissions, integration owner and exception queue | Staff approve consequential actions; tested rollback and manual service remain available |
| 3: Advanced governed | Forecast, detect patterns and optimise aggregate planning | Level 2 controls plus representative historical data, expert validation, monitoring and independent challenge | Demonstrated benefit against a non-AI baseline; uncertainty and failure limits are accepted by the accountable authority |

Level describes implementation complexity, not inherent safety. A simple chatbot can give harmful health advice; a sophisticated aggregate forecast can be comparatively low risk. Risk assessment is separate. An administration can obtain lasting value entirely at Level 1.

## A practical first 90 days

This is an illustrative work plan, not a delivery guarantee. Procurement and statutory reviews may require longer.

1. **Days 1–15: pick one service problem.** Name the accountable official and frontline reviewer. Measure current waiting time, rework, accessibility and service outcomes. Check whether clearer forms, search or ordinary automation would solve it more cheaply. Consult staff and affected service users.
2. **Days 16–30: prepare a bounded test.** Use public or synthetic records first. Select approved sources, a local-language test set and measurable acceptance thresholds. Complete privacy, security, procurement and legal review appropriate to risk. Record the escalation and shutdown paths.
3. **Days 31–60: shadow mode.** The system makes suggestions while existing service delivery continues. Review failures, minority-language performance and staff workload. Do not silently treat an unreviewed output as an official decision.
4. **Days 61–90: decide.** Compare against the baseline including reviewer time and full cost. Launch only within approved limits, extend the test if evidence is insufficient, or stop. Publish a proportionate service notice and provide a working human route if citizens encounter AI.

Start with one of these: a cited public-service FAQ for staff, an approved-document briefing assistant, or a public procurement-document completeness checklist. Avoid starting with benefit eligibility, clinical triage, policing profiles, immigration decisions or safety-critical operational control.

## What this catalogue deliberately excludes

Do not use these patterns to automate arrests, sentencing, border admissibility, tax penalties, benefit denial, diagnoses, admissions, hiring or dismissal. Do not infer credibility, criminality, intelligence, health status or deservingness from faces, voices, accents or social-media profiles. Automated consequential decisions require a separate lawful, independently evaluated governance programme; a human rubber stamp is not meaningful oversight.
